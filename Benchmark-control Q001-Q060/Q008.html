Detailed Analysis

The screening was conducted against the standardized trait "Milk yield" (Trait ID: PP01010101). The results are ranked by the number of unique genes associated with both the plant and the trait, which serves as a primary indicator of potential biological relevance.

1. Top Candidates and Evidence Strength:

  • Ginseng (人参, fnp072) leads the list with 68 supporting genes and 239 supporting functional annotations. This high count suggests a broad, multi-target interaction network that could influence pathways related to lactation.
  • Aloe (芦荟, fnp059) and Codonopsis (党参, fnp020) follow closely, with 65 and 63 supporting genes, respectively. Codonopsis has the highest number of supporting records (576), indicating extensive documented interactions in the database.
  • The "Supporting Record Count" represents the total number of evidence entries (e.g., gene-annotation pairs) linking the plant to the trait. A higher count generally correlates with a more robust and multi-faceted evidence base.

2. Association Level Context:

  • All candidates are flagged with an "Association Level" of "potentially related." This classification is applied when the evidence is derived from computational network analysis, pathway enrichment, or shared gene associations, but lacks direct, high-confidence experimental validation (e.g., clinical trials or controlled animal studies) for improving milk yield.
  • The absence of a "rich_evidence_count" (enrichment-supported evidence) for all candidates further underscores that these are predictive associations requiring further investigation.

3. Data Distribution and Tiers:

  • The supporting gene count among the 117 candidates shows a long-tail distribution. The top 5 plants are supported by 58-68 genes, while many candidates in the lower tier are supported by fewer than 10 genes.
  • This tiered structure helps prioritize candidates for further research. High-tier candidates (e.g., top 20) with strong gene support are more promising starting points for formula development or experimental validation.

Conclusion and Recommendations

Conclusion:
The analysis successfully identified 117 natural plants with potential links to milk yield in cattle through shared gene and pathway associations. The evidence is primarily predictive and network-based.

Recommendations:

  1. Prioritize Experimental Validation: Focus initial in vitro or pilot animal studies on the top-tier candidates, especially Ginseng, Aloe, and Codonopsis, due to their stronger genetic support.
  2. Mechanistic Investigation: For the leading candidates, delve deeper into the specific supporting genes and annotations (e.g., KEGG pathways like "Prolactin signaling pathway" or "PPAR signaling pathway") to hypothesize their mode of action on mammary gland physiology or nutrient metabolism.
  3. Formula Exploration: Consider developing composite formulas that combine several mid-to-high-tier candidates (e.g., Lycium barbarum (枸杞子), Zingiber officinale (生姜)) which may have synergistic effects.
  4. Evidence Augmentation: Actively search for existing scientific literature or trial data on these plants' effects on ruminant lactation to complement the computational evidence presented here.
  5. Safety and Palatability Screening: Before any feeding trial, consult existing databases or literature on the safety and palatability of these plants for cattle to avoid adverse effects.

Next Steps: A follow-up analysis could focus on the specific biological pathways and genes shared between the top candidates and the milk yield trait to generate actionable mechanistic hypotheses.
